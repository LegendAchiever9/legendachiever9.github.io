Pokémon Run and Bun

Played in 2025


Elevator Pitch

Run and Bun is a fan-made Pokémon Emerald ROM hack that transforms Pokémon from an RPG into one of the most demanding puzzle-strategy games I've ever played. Under the Hardcore Nuzlocke ruleset, every battle becomes a carefully calculated problem that tests your knowledge, planning, and risk management while being utterly unique — no two players will ever have the same solution.

Content

Run and Bun is a fan-made Pokémon Emerald ROM hack that dramatically expands the original game. Although Emerald is a Generation III title, Run and Bun includes Pokémon and mechanics through Generation VIII, Mega Evolution from Generation VI, a completely redesigned AI system, extensive balance changes to moves and items, and roughly 400 required battles throughout its campaign. I'll be reviewing it as though it were its own standalone game, even though it is technically a fan project.

For this playthrough, I used the Hardcore Nuzlocke ruleset: you may only catch the first Pokémon encountered on each route, items cannot be used during battle, every major fight has a level cap, any Pokémon that faints is permanently lost, and losing a single battle means restarting the entire game from the beginning. 

That may sound like an exercise in frustration, but Run and Bun rarely feels unfair. There's an important distinction between hard and challenging games. Hard games make you think, "I can't do this." Challenging games make you think, "I did that wrong." While there are certainly a good number of brutally difficult fights, Run and Bun spends far more time in the latter category. Even when you're consistently underpowered relative to the power curve of the game — through a combination of weaker Pokémon, limited resources, or consecutive gauntlet battles — the game almost always leaves a solution somewhere to be found. The catch is that not only do you need to find that solution, but also accept it as your way to continue.

Even though the game is excellent on its own (and much more approachable to play), this review is specifically about playing Run and Bun as a Hardcore Nuzlocke, because that's where it becomes something entirely different. 

 

The gameplay may look like Pokémon battles, but that's actually a remarkably small percentage of the experience. Most of your time isn't spent inside Pokémon at all — it's spent in a calculator, discovering whether a battle is winnable before you ever click your first move. The game requires such an intense level of planning that you have to fully plot out every single battle from start to finish. My longest calculation for a single battle took sixteen hours. The calculator isn't there to tell you what to do, it's there to expose more information so that you can make an intelligent decision. The game fully embodies both the art and science of risk management. 

Fully learning the AI behavior becomes the main weapon the player has to conquer the otherwise insurmountable challenge of this game. You’ll have to learn the fundamental rules for what Pokémon will get sent out next after you defeat one and how the opponent selects what moves to use on every turn. All of this is captured in a 20 page document, detailing the algorithm that drives this process. Eventually I realized that I wasn't really playing a Pokémon game anymore. I was in a duel against the AI that was puppeteering the puzzle behind every fight.

Due to built in variance, some fights had branching outcomes where I had to plan for each one. Other battles I was able to find riskless lines, ones that would always go as I predicted on every single turn. Run and Bun is about probabilities, and you have to do everything in your power to put them on your side as much as possible. But like anything with randomness, even the best planner in the world needs some amount of luck to get all the way through this game. Sometimes the right decision isn't eliminating every risk, it's deciding which risk deserves your time. Should you spend an additional ten hours to try and reduce a 0.5% chance of failure to 0%? Learning when to spend more time calculating another line and when to accept the probabilities you’re left with becomes a skill in itself. 

 

Now that we’ve talked extensively about the puzzle aspect of the game where you choose how to invest your time, there is a macro level of strategy that exists for you to manage your resource consumption. These resources consist of your box of Pokémon and your consumable items. Let’s talk about the former.

Because of the rule that you can only catch the first Pokémon on every route, the order in which you encounter each of these Pokémon matters quite a bit. This is what we call encounter routing, pre-planning out what routes you’ll encounter in order to maximize the chance of getting a teammate that you want, or in some cases, need. I’ll talk about some specific examples in the spoiler section, but there are some Pokémon in this game that you will plan on trying to catch before you even start a run. On the flip side, there are other encounters that will absolutely be the last thing you want. Sometimes that will be true, but even the most disappointing encounters may just save your run later down the line... 

The other way Pokémon are used as a resource in this game is when you unfortunately opt in to sacrificing one of your own. Some battles are just too difficult to get through without intentionally losing a Pokémon, so using the wide pool of encounters you get wisely is another mechanic of the game. A particularly bad encounter might be just the tool you need for a specific fight, and getting more value from it afterwards is support that you will absolutely need. 

The latter resource we mentioned is how you manage your consumable items throughout a playthrough. There are three main items to factor in here: rare candies, heart scales, and TMs. Rare candies increase your Pokémon’s level by one, but in a game with level caps and “infinite candies” that can level you up without grinding, they may seem obsolete. That couldn’t be further from the truth though, because rare candies in Run and Bun allow you to bypass the level cap. For a brutal boss battle or gauntlet, sometimes that one extra level is enough to let you outspeed an opponent or turn a range into a confirmation. Heart Scales are also changed in this game, allowing the player a few options: use one to relearn a move on a Pokémon, use one to maximize an IV (individual value, these are a Pokémon’s stats) for a Pokémon, or spend three to change the nature of a Pokémon (the percentage modifier to two of the Pokémon’s stats). Since the IVs and nature of each encounter are random, heart scales give you the flexibility to shape Pokémon into their best form. This is essential for moments where you just need some higher numbers on a Pokémon to make a line work. Lastly, TMs are single use items that teach a Pokémon a new move which may be exactly what you need to solve certain battles. All of these items are extremely limited over the course of this incredibly long journey, and budgeting them correctly to leave enough for the Elite 4 finale is yet another skill that will get put to its paces.

This is why I said Run and Bun is a puzzle game where no two players will ever have the same solution. The Pokémon you encounter will be unique to your playthrough, with their stats and natures ever further diverging your box from that of another player. Even after watching other players beat the game, you almost never get to copy their line. Those observations simply become more information that helps you discover your own answer. It is an incredibly rare occurrence for the genre, and I haven’t seen anything else like it. 

Having said all that, I almost didn't even play Run and Bun. I watched two complete playthroughs of the game before deciding it was worth attempting because the game looked like far too much effort. Yet somewhere along the way, watching someone else solve these puzzles became compelling enough that I wanted to solve my own. After streaming my own playthrough, I watched the same thing happen to friends. There's something about Run and Bun that slowly pulls people in. It doesn't wow you straight away, but before you realize it, you may want to see if you can solve the puzzle yourself. 

 

Run and Bun is ultimately a game about resource and risk management. It is a game that tests your ability to achieve mastery under heavy constraints, with Pokémon simply being the language the game uses to deliver that evaluation. Every Pokémon you catch, every consumable item you use, every percentage of risk you accept, every hour you spend — they're all resources. Your knowledge of the game’s mechanics, your choices made throughout the game, and your luck all lead to more information that allows you, and you alone, to find the way forward.

Pain Points

Accessibility is the biggest pain point, starting with the fact that you need almost 30 years of Pokémon knowledge. Documentation lives exclusively outside of the game which presents an enormous burden to entry, I wish the information lived inside the game itself.

 

Documentation errors, ambiguity, or omitted cases are another big issue, especially when you’re 400 hours deep into a run. No blame on anyone here, this game was a massive undertaking to create and the will of the community alone is why we even have so much documentation to begin with. I point this out only to contextualize what the game would need to truly be able to stand on its own.

 

Lastly this isn't necessarily even an issue, but a fight taking 16 hours to calculate is certainly an investment. Successful runs of this game will easily take months to complete, so playing Run and Bun with this ruleset is certainly not something I would recommend for everyone.

 

I actually cannot recommend this ruleset for Run and Bun, despite how impactful the game was for me. On its own, the game is made with outstanding quality and is a joy to play. But the truly incomparable experience comes from the ruleset, and it is simply too much of a commitment for most people to ever try. But for those willing to put in the effort to try, I've never played a game quite like it.

Music

Since this is a fanmade game, all of the music is actually unchanged from Pokémon Emerald. I also don’t want to take too much time away from the story of Run and Bun which I view as the primary experience that this game aims to create. As a result, I won’t do my usual soundtrack analysis for this game — if you’re looking for that, I’ll do a deep dive in my Pokémon Alpha Sapphire review! Instead, I’ll just lightly touch on a few of my favorite themes to give you some excellent background listening for the saga below.

 

“Littleroot Town” is the theme for the starting area of the game and it absolutely nails the longing, nostalgic feeling you associate with home. The pizzicato strings and lone flute over a synth bass create such a lighthearted atmosphere that is still packed with emotion. The strings then take over the melody supported by a fuller instrumentation before an electric piano continues on with a new chord progression. This all wraps up with the strings playing the original line but up an octave before looping. A simple idea executed perfectly, this song is so lovely.

 

“Petalburg City” is another home theme of sorts, as this is where the protagonist’s father lives as the resident Gym Leader. This tune is upbeat and just instills pure joy into the listener. A light trumpet and bass kick off the main idea before a jazzy, syncopated rhythm plays below the melody. The fast moving instrumentation and rhythm drive the song to create a sense of energy and action. The track is just so fun you might not want to leave this city.

Music

“Elite Four Battle” for Emerald takes the cake as one of my favorite Elite Four themes. The emblematic trumpets of this generation immediately set the stakes with a classic fast moving synth bassline. They don’t let up either, as the entire melody is driven by powerful brass with harmonies in the strings. 0:44 brings in the crowd with a clapping melody way before Sword and Shield brought us into the arena. This syncopated rhythm is so iconic and gives the player a much needed breather from the nonstop brass. It doesn’t let up in intensity, it rather uses the emptier space to create a moment of buildup before we get blitzed down by the rapid trumpet melody again.

 

Without spoiling anything, “Victory! (Champion)” has always been my personal favorite champion victory theme. So even with my uncertain destiny when it comes to Pokémon Run and Bun, this song deserves a highlight. After the combative trumpets dominate the melody for the Elite Four and Champion battles, hearing them erupt in a fanfare for you never gets old. The melody at 0:08 is nothing short of heroic, with a beautifully full and cheerful march to celebrate everything that becoming champion means. The french horn takes over the melody with a flurry of different countermelodies keeping it fresh for the ear. The rolling snare brings quite the formality to this tune, but the melody and optimism that exude from this are palpable. No matter if it’s your first Pokémon game or the end of a journey, this is how you close out a true adventure.

 

Not only does Pokémon Emerald have my favorite Victory theme, but “Ending / Credits” is without any doubt my favorite credits roll. This is a heartfelt love letter to the player for putting their heart into the world of Pokémon. A repeated synth pattern drives this heartwrenching forward while a beautiful piano solo plays. 0:30 brings in the next idea with a higher octave with some faster moving runs that cascade back into the central motif. I love that this piece is in tempo rubato, where the music can take its time to slow down for heavier moments. Flutes and a tuba are emulated by synths at 1:13 for the next phrase while electronic supporting synths flutter around as countermelodies. The tuba drops back down to the bassline as the string section plays in unison to lead into 1:54. And don’t you know it, we have to take the final phrase with none other than the brass section. A trumpet fanfare and french horn lead erupt after a drum roll and cymbal crash. This isn’t a march though, this is a final hurrah packed with emotion and excitement. The strings get the melody last more time as we come down from the climax of the piece into 2:36. If you listen for the soft trumpet here you can hear a reprise of the original Pokémon theme, an absolutely perfect detail for “The End”.Backdropping the entire credits sequence with a montage of every Pokémon that you’ve befriended along the way is also a stroke of genius that further augments the emotional impact of this piece. All of this comes together in a way that elicits an emotional response from the player. This was your journey, and as bittersweet as it may be, this is its ending.

Content

This spoiler section will be a little different than most. There isn't some plot twist to preserve or ending to dissect. The real story of Run and Bun isn't written by the game at all, it's written by the player. The hardcore Nuzlocke ruleset actually began as a storytelling device. Permanent death, limited encounters, and starting over from the beginning weren't created just to make Pokémon harder; they exist because they naturally create stories. Every run becomes a unique adventure that no one else can replicate. This is my story of Run and Bun attempt seven.

 

The second gym battle against Roxanne was already planned to have two intentional sacrifices. It wasn't clean, but after hours of calculations it was the best line I could find against her Zygarde 10% and demonic Lunatone. But everyone has a plan until they get hit for the first time, or in this case,  I was critically hit three separate times — a statistical anomaly in a game where crits are only 1/16. By the end of the battle I had lost four Pokémon instead of two. Another failed run, or so I had thought. Run and Bun has this impalpable way of giving you just enough to continue, even when you think that there are no options left. So I trudged on.

 

A deathless third gym became my new personal best. Soon after, I survived the infamous Cycling Road Rival by accepting a roughly 15% chance to immediately lose the run because no safer line existed. The run lived, but Nidoqueen, my hero in the previous battle, did not. That was the one of many lessons Run and Bun kept teaching me: sometimes the perfect play involves accepting imperfect odds.

 

As the run progressed, something strange happened. The run that should have died after Roxanne kept becoming stronger. Strong encounter routing and good fortune rewarded me with Pokémon like Primarina, Overqwil, Alolan Muk, Hisuian Arcanine, Aerodactyl, Golisopod. I even got lucky hits with Beedrill and Ursaluna with Guts. Every new encounter felt like another puzzle piece. None of them were guaranteed, and no other player could have assembled the same box that I had. That is one of the beauties of Run and Bun. Anyone can show how they solved their puzzles, but I still had to solve mine.

 

Beedrill, a.k.a. LL Dreebi (with my anagram nickname theme) is an excellent Pokémon due to its mega evolution, which turns it into an incredibly fast damage dealer. This led me all the way through a deathless Winona in the sixth gym. That brought me to Lilycove City for the next rival battle, one that took 6 hours to calculate. The end of this battle was a perfect example of Run and Bun somehow giving exactly what is needed for the run. If the opposing Galarian Articuno missed Hurricane, I had the choice to save my Honchkrow and sacrifice a different Pokémon instead. It ended up happening at a 30% chance, so Honchrow lived and my journey continued. 

 

Up on reaching the top of Mt. Pyre, I had low hopes. This was a  tag battle, so I could only bring in three of my own Pokémon. I just couldn’t find a riskless line, even with the plan to intentionally sacrifice two of my own Pokémon (the Overqwil and Alolan Muk that we routed for earlier) by using Explosion to wipe out the opponent’s side of the field. Not only that, but I had to use my only Normal Gem and Custap Berry to execute this bombastic plan. This resulted in a 14/16 chance to win the battle; and by some miracle, I made it through again.

 

With Pyre Tag out of the way, it was into Magma Hideout. This dungeon has permanent Magma Storm, which does 1/8th of your active Pokémon’s health every turn and prevents you from switching out. It was at this point, three months into my playthrough, that I made a decision: in victory or defeat, this would be my last attempt at Run and Bun. The game had already cemented itself among the greatest gaming experiences I'd ever had, but the time commitment had become enormous. If this run ended, so would my journey. That realization brought an unexpected calm, all that was left was to keep doing everything I could, one battle, and one move at a time. The battle itself demanded two planned sacrifices before everything came down to a single turn. Claydol had to survive switching into an attack from Naganadel, a sequence that boiled down to a 4% chance to lose the entire run instantly. I pressed A.

 

Sludge Wave. The one move that always let me survive, regardless of crit. One Explosion and a calculated endgame later, Maxie was defeated.

 

Then there was only one fight left between me and the seventh gym, Aqua Admin Matt. The fight went all according to plan, with the final Pokémon being Mega Gyarados. That is, until I accidentally switched out my Hydreigon instead of using U-Turn. My heart sank. Three months of attempt seven, potentially evaporated due to one misclick. Could I reset the battle? In theory yes, but what if the misclick had somehow served me well, would I have reset it in that situation? It’s hard to say, but in my opinion, in order to keep the run’s integrity, I had to play with the mistake. But just like before, every time I thought the run was over, Run and Bun asked me to solve one more puzzle, one more time.

 

The seventh gym was a celebration, not only of my run up until this point, but a moment to share the magic of this game with my friends. I fully streamed the entire gym, inviting friends and coworkers alike to participate in the experience that Run and Bun provides. A few people even started their own attempts after this stream, something about watching this game for enough makes you feel like you want a piece of the action. The seventh gym is exclusively double battles, the hardest type of fight in the game, until the gym leaders themselves. Instead, you challenge them in two consecutive single battles with no healing in between. This fight also houses the community named “Heist”, an optional objective that requires you to steal an item in the middle of the battle. This theft is effectively required in order to have a reasonable shot at the remainder of the game, so I went into the gym with everything I had. 

 

With all of the gym battles completed and my line for the heist ready, it was time to challenge Tate & Liza. The only way I had to steal this item was to fully stall out Tate’s Hoopa until it had almost no moves left to use. After that, I could use the Role Play move on my Kecleon to copy Hoopa’s ability, Magician. This allows the user to take items after dealing contact damage to another Pokémon. Myself and the stream chat counted out each move used, and the stall was complete. Everything was ready, so I clicked Role Play, turning Kecleon into a Psychic type. At that moment though, time seemed to freeze as the text “Leader Tate withdrew Hoopa” appeared on my screen. Switch AI, or the ability for the computer to switch out its own Pokémon, is incredibly rare in Run and Bun. It is effectively impossible in double battles, and only feasible in specific situations for single battles. After six consecutive double battles, this case had slipped my mind — even though I knew this fact, it hadn’t registered in any of my hours of calculating because of how niche this mechanic was. Just like that, the plan was in shambles. My turned-Psychic type Kelcon was now face to face with Zoroark which had the move Pursuit, holding a Dark Gem. My plan needed Kecleon alive not only for the heist, but also for the incoming battle against Liza. Disaster had finally struck, and it felt like the run was over. But I wasn’t ready to give up yet. I spent an hour calculating a new line from this position and even hopped on Pokémon Showdown with a friend to test the interaction between Sucker Punch and Struggle. After all this, I had a new line that would work as long as I could dodge Kecleon immediately dying. With baited breath I selected my choice, and Kecleon lived. The heist was complete. To this day, I don't think any moment better captures what Run and Bun is all about. 

 

The final split became a test of endurance. The infamous Space Tag required finding a completely new solution after losing Kecleon (my last Pokémon with Fake Out) in the Heist. With my tag battle ally Steven fully out of Pokémon, I had only found one way out in all of my planning. Remember Honchkrow from earlier? The one who lived only because of the 30% chance for Hurricane to miss? Equipped with a scarf and my best wishes, Honchkrow had one turn to change the tides of battle. Moving first with the Choice Scarf, Honchkrow set up Tailwind and immediately went down as an intended sacrifice. This enabled the rest of my team to cinch the victory.

 

Fight after fight, the power of my box and my growth as a player continued to show up. Even in the ruthless Seafloor Cavern, a dungeon where the opponents have permanent Aurora Veil, my team and I stood the test. My encounter for this area felt destined — with several Elite 4 contenders like Absol, Weavile, Grimmsnarl, and Sneasler constituting over a 60% chance to find. So I was filled with utter dismay when Hisuian Samurott appeared on my screen. I felt like I needed a boost to take this run to the end, but the game disagreed. Like everything else Run and Bun had taught me for those five months, I had to accept what I was given, and press on.

 

Some battles rewarded my effort with perfectly riskless solutions. Others refused and forced me to grapple with a fascinating question: how much time is reducing a 1% chance to 0% actually worth? Managing your time becomes just as important as managing your Pokémon. The final required fight before the Elite 4 is Victory Road's Winstrate Vito which captures this sentiment well. This battle took me sixteen straight hours of calculations before I finally discovered a line that worked. Not sixteen hours playing the game mind you, sixteen hours staring at a calculator, exploring thousands of possibilities, trying to reduce risk by fractions of a percent. 

 

Upon reaching the Elite Four, I already had a completely riskless line to get through it. The only problem was that it wasn't mine. I could use my remaining scales to bring an identical Elite 4 team as a previous runner. The path to victory was right in front of me, but for some reason, I didn’t take it. I was filled with determination, not only to win, but to win my way. The community has a tradition of trying to clear the Elite Four with a Pokémon that no previous champion had used. Only 86 players had completed the game before me, and I wanted to become the 87th while also bringing a unique Pokémon into the Hall of Fame. 

 

After hours and hours of planning, I had an answer. It would require the very last optional items, so I had to do all of the battles that guarded them. I went forward, finding one of my favorite lines in the game. In a double battle, my Latios At 229 HP barely didn’t show Florges a knockout with Moonblast, which capped out at 228 damage. After securing the takedown on the opposing Swalot however, I needed to bait Moonblast into Latios so I could safely switch out into Beedrill. The way I accomplished this was to pre-poison Latios. After the poison chip, Latios went down by 1/16th of its maximum HP, which brought it down to 192 HP. And wouldn’t you know it, somehow, some way, the minimum roll of Moonblast from Florges was 192. This meant that I was always showing an exact knockout to Florges, forcing it to Moonblast into that slot which could safely switch. The elegance of Run and Bun’s design is hard to understate, and moments like this have the ability to abstract you away from the hours of effort, instead focusing on the accomplishment and satisfaction of finding lines like that.

 

With that done, I had all the optional items and my team ready for the Elite 4. I made it. In success or failure, I was at the doorstep to the champion in the world’s most difficult Pokémon game. In what almost felt like a written script, my final team member for the Elite 4, my unique Pokémon that no one had brought to the Hall of Fame before, was none other than Hisuian Samurott. The Pokémon that could have been a Sneasler, the one that I hadn’t brought to any battles since the encounter. Something that I thought was actively lowering my chance to win, ended up being the Pokémon that made it possible for me to bring a unique into the Hall of Fame. Sometimes Run and Bun, and life in general, will surprise you if you give them the chance. 

 

I won't recount every turn of the Elite Four here. I streamed and recorded the entire finale here, so I'd much rather let you experience those battles as they happened. My unique Pokémon made preparing for this Elite 4 tougher than using a tried and true strategy, but this was my run, my journey. And I was prepared to end it that way. Even to that point, my run only existed because of everyone who came before me. Watching players like PChal and SmallAnt didn't teach me how to beat Run and Bun, rather it taught me how to think about the game. 

 

Those runs taught me the essentials of cooking, the fundamentals on how different flavors can work together. Run and Bun itself gave me the ingredients, no matter how lacking they looked, to create a premium dish. But with all said and done, I was the chef. The calculations, the risks I took, the steers after mistakes, the unconventional lines, and the final Elite Four team were uniquely mine. No future player will ever recreate that exact journey because no one can ever have that exact box, those resources, or those decisions. 

 

That's what makes Run and Bun brilliantly special. At first glance it looks like one of the hardest Pokémon games ever made. And it is. But in the end, the game is really about mastery and determination. After six months and more than four hundred hours, I actually did it. The challenge that seemed insurmountable, to triumph over the grandest puzzle of all, a game that only 86 other people had ever managed to beat. With help, with luck, or with skill, I did it. And I did it my way, with my story. And that is what the Nuzlocke is all about.

Summary

Run and Bun isn't really about Pokémon, it's about testing your mettle to solve problems that look impossible. It transforms one of gaming's most familiar RPGs into a puzzle and strategy game where knowledge, planning, persistence, and risk management become just as valuable as your Pokémon companions.

I can't recommend a hardcore nuzlocke playthrough of this game to most people, the barrier to entry is enormous and the investment required is even larger. But if you're willing to face this challenge, I genuinely think it is the most unique gaming experience anyone can have.
