Children of the Sun

Played in 2026


Elevator Pitch

Children of the Sun is a puzzle shooter with a twist: you are the bullet. Tasked with eliminating a cult that killed your family, you’re out for revenge by using your telekinetic abilities to steer the bullet through each target with bombastic spectacle or ruthless efficiency — the choice is yours.

Content

Every level of the game begins as what appears to be a standard sniper puzzle, but the instant you fire the rifle, the game transforms into something entirely different. Rather than controlling the shooter, you're now in a first person perspective controlling the bullet itself. This single camera change transforms the game from a sniper simulator into an action puzzle where you need to redirect the bullet after hitting each target. The premise alone is already so memorable and what sold me on the game in the first place; this is a totally new approach for the genre and captured my attention immediately. That novelty isn't even the game's greatest strength. Instead, it dramatically expands the bounds of what a sniper puzzle can be. From the very first level, the game opens your mind to the idea of first steering the bullet into a gas canister, enabling you to re-aim your next shot from a new vantage point. This isn’t a game about your pinpoint marksmanship from the first shot. In reality, every level becomes a route-planning puzzle long before you ever pull the trigger.

 

While eliminating every enemy is the primary way to progress through the story, each level comes with its own hidden objective and a score for your performance. The levels do a solid job of providing a unique set piece or new gimmick to keep them feeling fresh, while the alternative objectives and scoring motivate you to find the perfect sequence. This concept of perfection is quite subjective throughout the game though: while the scoring system rewards speed and headshots, I found myself chasing something slightly different. Not even from the score, but more so from my own interpretation of what a good run was. Sometimes that meant minimizing redirects, other times it meant chaining together stylish explosions or finding a solution that simply felt smooth. The game elegantly allows the player to define their own metrics of what success looks like. As a result, I was often immediately going back into levels to try and do just a bit better than before.

 

When starting a level, I would frequently fire a bullet to scout out the level and see what my options would be for my eventual “winning run”. This highlights the game’s strength in rewarding your planning in a gratifying way. After several preliminary shots giving me a solid grasp of the level, I would come up with a full chain of execution that, when completed successfully, would grant me the highest satisfaction. That being said, not everything in this game is about planning. Mapping out the entire assassination before firing has its glory, but some of my favorite moments came from turning mid-flight mistakes into new discoveries. The “bullet controlling” part of the game has a great way of creating tense moments in the action as you improvise an entirely different solution than you had planned for. 

 

Children of the Sun rocks a unique gritty, surrealist artstyle. Intensely saturated neon hues along with a lo-fi and slightly psychedelic environment whisks you away to the likes of 70s indie film, but the game never sacrifices clarity to achieve this aesthetic. The graphic novel styled backgrounds are always darker and less crisp than the bright highlights of your targets and key elements (like explosive barrels or birds) within the level — this enables the game to maintain a highly accessible visual language for the player.

 

The story for the game follows the player character as a child whose family is disrupted by a cult. It’s brutal, uncomfortable, and certainly doesn’t end happily. Trigger warning for pretty gruesome imagery, I personally did not enjoy the story, though I don’t think you’re really meant to. I was fully in it for the puzzles and the game delivers on those quite well.

Pain Points

The primary pain point for me in this game is actually in the soundtrack, so I’ll cover that more in the music section below. 

 

Other than that, there isn’t too much to complain about. With the relatively short playtime, nothing overstays its welcome for too long. Even the levels I didn’t enjoy as much had some amount of satisfaction in completing the hidden objectives.

Music

Most of the soundtrack is atmospheric as you walk around the level before you shoot, featuring environmental sounds and the occasional droning synth. Once you fire, the soundtrack fully drops away in favor of sound effects to highlight the action — the elongated deep tones of slow motion or mechanical whirring of an accelerating bullet. All of this works quite well for the overall vibe that the game is going for, but that means we don’t have any tracks to review!

 

The other aspect of the OST is where this lands in the camp of intolerable for me personally. Cutscenes and the level completion theme have a grungy vibe with heavy distortions that aren’t inherently bad but are physically painful for my ears. While thematically fitting for the brutal nature of the game’s edgy story and visuals, it was past a threshold for me where I really cannot listen to it.

Content

One of the levels that cemented Children of the Sun as a game worth playing was ‘Open Mic Night in Hell’. This is one of the most puzzle-y levels in the game, which you may not expect when you’re greeted with a massive facade of an apartment complex and no targets in sight. The secret objective is aptly named “he is blocking the entrance” which not so subtly hints you towards the only way into the level, killing a target in the front door. This initial kill then gives you your first real look at the full setting, a wide open courtyard with around 20 targets —  the world is now your oyster. Some players like myself may immediately go for the explosive barrels accompanying several targets directly in the middle of the area, resulting in a massive explosion while they scour for where the plethora of other targets are located. They may pick off a few more before running out of re-aims or narrowly missing a shot, but then you get right back in and try again. Through the front door, this time taking a new route to assess where all of the targets are in preparation for finding the perfect run. This loop of discovery and mastery is the bread and butter of the game, and few levels get you better into that rhythm than this one. 

Another mission that highlights the game’s ability to bring out your inner planner is ‘Surveil the Dead’ with the hidden objective “it's been a long time”. This optional addition is quite simple: complete the level after 45 seconds have elapsed, which is when more enemies will have spawned into the level. Those who aren’t tempted by high scores can just play the mission before that timer, while others who are pursuing either a leaderboard position or just looking for the most satisfying approach like myself are met with a playground of options. There are so many ways to tackle this level, it really brought out my inner planner. I spent so much time probing different initial targets to kill, switching up the order of execution, moving to unique starting positions, and it was an excellent amalgamation of everything the game had taught me so far. And the best part, the planning paid off! Not only did my run earn a decently high score, but most importantly, I felt like I had come up with an optimal way to complete the level according to my standards.

 

The final mission, The Temple, is a really cool concept that is executed quite well; it’s just a bit too repetitive. It is by far the longest level in terms of total area and number of targets, and since it has no checkpoints you’ll be replaying this one several times before you succeed. What exacerbates that is the introduction of barriers: forcefields that instantly stop your bullet and only drop until you eliminate every target in a set area of the level. Only once you drop the barrier you move on to the next section of the level, and when you fail here you’ll have to replay the entire mission again. It is a really well made level from a layout perspective though, with a highly nonlinear structure and a multitude of options for stylish and satisfying routing.

Summary

Children of the Sun demonstrates that a single, inspired mechanic can deliver an extraordinary experience when every system exists to explore it from a different angle. Rather than asking the player whether or not they can eliminate every target, it presents a far more interesting premise: how elegantly can you do it? What drives you to go through a level one more time; is it trying to top the leaderboard, maybe finding a creative way to navigate the area, or perhaps just a run that feels right? That freedom to define your own perfect run is what kept pulling me back into missions long after I'd already completed them. This game creates space for players to create their own action movie moment yet stays streamlined enough to not be repetitive or frustrating. Even with a more niche take on the story and soundtrack that weren’t for me, the catchy premise and compelling level design were more than enough to keep me around for the whole journey.
