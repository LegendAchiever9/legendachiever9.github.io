Hollow Knight

First Played in 2017


Elevator Pitch

Hollow Knight set the modern standard for the Metroidvania genre by crafting an experience centered around one of gaming's most powerful feelings: curiosity. The interconnected world is packed with secrets, meaningful rewards, and beautiful environments, while its exceptionally tight controls make both combat and exploration satisfying to master.

Content

Hollow Knight is a Metroidvania set in the ruined kingdom of Hallownest. It combines the genre's familiar ingredients — an interconnected map to explore, ability-gated progression, combat, platforming, upgrades — into a remarkably cohesive package. The mechanics alone, though, don’t fully capture what makes this game so special.

For me, Hollow Knight has one of the best possible deliveries of atmosphere and exploration that you can find in the genre, and even in gaming as a whole. It is a densely packed, unique experience that leaves a lasting impression, wrapped in phenomenal controls that always feel fair. Every system, set piece, character, theme, and detail contributes to an overall feeling of wonder with a hint of melancholy.

 

The world of Hallownest is large and diverse, yet never feels empty. Areas aren't simply there to connect one important location to another; exploring every screen itself is the main event. There are secrets to find, enemies to fight, upgrades to discover, lore to uncover, shortcuts to open, and quiet spaces to appreciate. The game creates a constant desire in the player to ask what is around the corner and find a way to get there.

Importantly, Hollow Knight doesn't rely on constantly putting something spectacular in front of you to create that feeling. The world is designed so that just walking through it sparks interest in the player — a strange wall, a suspiciously empty room, a murmuring sound nearby, the sparkle of a new item just out of reach, or some place that remains unfilled on your map. The game repeatedly provides a lead that will make you eager to follow it.

 

A key element to exploration is that the reward must feel compelling enough to encourage the player to continue the journey of discovery. Almost every screen in Hollow Knight has something that makes it worth visiting. It might be mechanically useful, narratively interesting, visually striking, or simply pleasant to experience. 

Hollow Knight teaches you how to be curious within its world. Early discoveries establish that walls can hide things, that environmental details can provide clues, and that seemingly insignificant spaces can contain something worthwhile. As you become more familiar with the game's visual language, you begin looking for those clues yourself. This synergizes well with traversal upgrades throughout the game. Hollow Knight follows the traditional, ability-gated progression of the Metroidvania genre, but it uses those abilities particularly well. A new movement ability turns previously inaccessible terrain into a list of possibilities that you’ll immediately want to revisit to see what new secrets you have access to.

This also makes backtracking much more enjoyable than it can be in other Metroidvanias. You're often not simply returning to an old area because you have been instructed to collect an upgrade there. You're revisiting somewhere because you placed a map marker hours before to note something suspicious, or have a new ability that might open the door to more secrets.

 

All of this exploration could become tedious if movement and combat weren’t satisfying. To be perfectly clear, Hollow Knight is the tightest-controlling Metroidvania that I have ever played. There is no momentum, so you can turn immediately, even midair, and precisely control jump height. That precision is a huge part of why Hollow Knight feels fair and satisfying. Hitboxes are clear and enemy attack patterns readable so nothing feels cheap or unfair.

The game doesn't require an enormous moveset to create interesting possibilities. Instead, a relatively small number of mechanics interact with one another in increasingly complex ways. The same basic tools can be used differently depending on the situation, and mastery comes from understanding those possibilities rather than simply acquiring more buttons. A certain mechanic alone (that we’ll discuss later) provides a large amount of depth that significantly changes how the player views both movement and combat.

 

Hollow Knight also gives the player some room to define how they want to approach its challenges through the Charm system. These items provide unique effects, but you can only equip a limited number at a time. Some charms make exploration more convenient, while others encourage different approaches to combat. You can optimize your loadout for a particular challenge, lean into a playstyle you enjoy, or equip something simply because you find it entertaining. 

Hollow Knight is constantly giving you tools without insisting that there is only one correct way to use them. The limited slots also give your choices some weight, as you’ll have to make situational trade-offs when deciding which charms to equip. This system is so effective, its popularization as a result of Hollow Knight has made this a staple throughout the genre.

 

Another huge reason why the game is so impactful is the atmosphere created by the world of Hallownest itself. Every area has a distinct visual identity, soundscape, collection of enemies, architecture, and mood. The game can move between beauty, oppression, serenity, danger, and melancholy without losing cohesion. That variety is important because it sustains the player's interest throughout a large world, making them invested in where they're going next.

The kingdom of Hallownest is decaying and abandoned, but it doesn't feel lifeless. The environments suggest a history that existed long before you ever arrived. The creatures and characters inhabiting them make each region feel like part of a larger ecosystem, naturally prompting questions about what happened.

The overall tone of Hollow Knight is melancholic and isolating. The entire game takes place underground in the remains of a kingdom that is nearing its end. But even in that, the lingering beauty of the world and the charisma of its residents are endearing enough to invoke a sense of comfort. Even with an ever-present air of repression, the world has so much personality that it creates an incredibly effective atmosphere.

 

The game seldom stops to explain the world to you; rather, much of its history is communicated through environmental details, brief exchanges of dialogue, visual storytelling, journal entries, and events you encounter while exploring. Having a silent protagonist also lends itself to the sense of attachment you develop toward the world — the characters and their voices give Hallownest its personality and make the journey feel less solitary. Hollow Knight gives you enough information to understand the broad shape of its story while leaving room to interpret the details for yourself. The ambiguity isn't always easy to follow, but it encourages the same curiosity that drives its exploration. You aren't simply waiting for the game to explain what happened; instead, you're actively piecing together threads to form the larger narrative..

 

Hollow Knight's art style is immediately recognizable. Sharply inked line art defines the primary playable plane while softer lines, contours, and blurs in the background and foreground create depth without distracting from the 2D gameplay. This provides another massive lift in making an otherwise stagnant world spring to life. The combination of the sound design, art direction, soundtrack, and creativity of the world itself is what makes the experience so immersive. Each element reinforces the others to create something deeply memorable.

The music deserves particular credit here. Hollow Knight's soundtrack is not simply good music placed over good environments. Its themes give individual areas their own emotional identities while leitmotifs create connections between characters, locations, and story. I'll go much deeper into that later, in what will likely be the longest music section I'll ever write.

 

So we have extremely precise combat and platforming that is satisfying and gives the player control. There is a large, detailed world to explore, ripe with secrets that are rewarding to uncover. There's also an incredibly charming cast of creatively designed bugs that inhabit a wide variety of areas, each with their own unique moods.

As a player, all of this gets you invested in the journey and actively curious about what is up ahead. It’s not that any specific concept is revolutionary, but each system is well-polished and lets the player know that exploring will be worth it. Everything reinforces the core feeling of wonder that Hollow Knight delivers in an exceptional way.

Pain Points

Hollow Knight isn't perfect, but for me it comes quite close. 

 

The earliest parts of the game are relatively linear and gated by the need to gather enough money, which can leave it feeling a bit slow. But once the floodgates are open, the game absolutely takes off and doesn’t slow down. 

 

Related, map markers are a great way to track your curiosity, especially throughout the early game when you don’t have many upgrades but want to remember points of interest for later. However, since they cost money at a time when you'll need it most, newer players may find it difficult to afford them early on.

 

Also, while I think Hollow Knight's narrative ambiguity stems from the strength of its environmental storytelling and attention to detail, players who want a more direct narrative may find themselves doing more interpretive work than they would prefer.

Overall, these are relatively small complaints because they don't undermine what the game does best.

Music

The music of Hollow Knight is an absolute masterpiece, featuring primarily strings and piano. From intense runs and soaring violin solos to somber piano passages and operatic arias, the game features an extraordinary range that is appropriate for the scope of the world it accompanies. As the studio's debut title, most of the soundtrack was created using virtual instruments, with the exception of a viola and a soprano vocalist, both of which play key roles in a few tracks.

 

The soundtrack is extremely important in establishing the atmosphere, but it is equally important to the narrative. A leitmotif is a short musical phrase or musical idea that allows the listener to associate it with a character or concept — think of the “Imperial March” from ‘Star Wars’ whenever Darth Vader is on screen. While it's debatable whether Hollow Knight's themes actually use leitmotifs, since they aren't always explicitly tied to a single character or idea, I consider them as such, especially when you take the full story into account.



“Hollow Knight” is the title theme and therefore the first track you'll hear. This is important because it introduces the primary leitmotif of the entire game. The key of C minor gives this piece a sad tone, and the instrumentation pushes that even further: a sorrowful piano plays the melody while the left hand wanders over wistful chords. This first five-note phrase from the piano is the leitmotif which ends on D, the second note of the scale (this will be important later, don’t worry I won’t get more technical!). After the first idea concludes, the piano repeats the theme, this time doubled by a longing, melancholic viola. The overall timbre of this track is dark but even the nearly tragic sound brings about some sort of peace when you listen to it. It's an exceptional tone-setter for the world you'll soon find yourself in, both atmospherically and by giving you an innate feel for the main theme. What it exactly represents, though, I’ll tell you later!



This next one isn’t actually on the soundtrack, but it is an important musical moment that plays during the intro cutscene (it starts at 1:03 in this video if you want to follow along). After some chords way up in the piano’s high register, we hear the same leitmotif return at 1:28, this time with the first note played once instead of twice. As the player character jumps down and the cutscene ends, the final note of this phrase is actually the tonic, C, or the first note of the scale (again, this is important for later) providing a sense of finality and resolution to this moment.



“Enter Hallownest” is in the OST but isn’t actually something you ever hear in game. The only place we've heard this song is in a gameplay trailer, but I love it and it brings the leitmotif into play so let’s jump in. A moving piano line, violin crescendo, and harp run lead us into 0:05, where the viola enters with a beautiful rendition of the main theme, accompanied by a stylish pitch bend at 0:14 and doubled by the harp. Even with the same melody as the title theme, this song is so much more hopeful and energetic. This comes partly from the dynamism of the moving bass line, but some of that feeling also stems from the key, in this case A minor. This gives the line a much brighter tone, matching the reflective optimism that the piece instills.

 

The tension builds at 0:23 into a climax at 0:27 as the high strings and piano repeat the central melody (same motif again) while the lower strings keep the pulse and the flutes dance around with fast runs. 0:43 mirrors the first buildup, but instead of exploding into a high energy section we get a lovely reprise at 0:49. A viola solo gives us the main idea again, with harp runs fluttering as accompaniment before the flute joins in to double the melody. Finally, a string tremolo and an ascending line from the flute and harp bring the piece to a dramatic end with a bass drum hit. It’s a shame we don’t get to hear this in the game itself, but the appearance of the leitmotif in this track is all we need to establish its importance before examining it further in the spoiler section.

Music

“Greenpath” is an important area theme because it plays when you first arrive in the area, after the dark and dreary passages of the Forgotten Crossroads. This piece captures the natural beauty of the lush greenery that still finds a home amid the stagnant wastelands of the kingdom.

 

The track starts out with a peaceful ostinato figure in the harp, which acts as the driving force of the piece. "The harp also plays an arpeggio figure, which the marimba mirrors peacefully. The viola plays the melody, while the English horn enters at 0:41 with the countermelody. At 1:02 we hear pizzicato arpeggios in the cello to add some texture while flute flourishes and the glockenspiel carry the melodic line. The central theme undergoes some variations with new instrumentation and different supporting figures to keep things calm but adventurous. At 1:51, string stabs and pizzicato figures build up into next section while the bassoon plays a muted melody that gradually crescendos. 2:25 is the climax of the piece where syncopated violin, cello, and now percussion enter to provide a forward drive to the piece. The original instruments that first established the peaceful theme continue to play the melody ,creating a unique blend of serenity and action that inspires both comfort and a desire to explore.



Once more I have another short phrase that brings us a new leitmotif that is crucial for the next theme. The ‘Elegy for Hallownest’ is musical another moment in the intro cutscene (0:03 in this video if you want to listen) that quietly introduces a core musical idea that will recur throughout the game.



“Hornet” serves as both the character theme and boss theme for Hornet. Right away, we have a fast-moving rhythmic line in ¾, which makes the piece feel even more light-footed, just like a dancer. This full string composition builds into a quick breakdown before the melody enters at 0:16 as a violin solo. Listen to those first five notes. They may be sped up and in a different key, but they're the exact progression from ‘Elegy for Hallownest.. Consider that the elegy is dedicated to the Pale King and Hornet is his daughter, and we’ve found ourselves a leitmotif; the pale leitmotif as I’ll call it.

 

Rapid high string upswells fit the intensity of a boss fight like at 0:23 where they soon let the viola take over the main line. We then get a call-and-response pattern between the violins and violas all while the lower strings keep driving the piece forward with constant eighth notes. The cellos keep the piece grounded and serious, even amid the elegant melodies. The bridge at 1:00 instantly creates tension as the high strings are accented by cello runs, opposite to what we’ve heard so far. This is followed by an insanely awesome piano section at 1:20, which uses polyrhythm. "This particular pattern is what we'd call a 'four over three' pattern: we have rapid sixteenth-note groupings even though the time signature is still in three. This creates an almost jarring, chaotic feeling that is perfect for the tense moments of a demanding battle. This piece is awesome.



“Reflection” is the theme that plays whenever you rest at a bench, tram, or hot spring. When I talk about Hollow Knight being a masterpiece of atmosphere, it’s tracks like this that drive that point home. High-pitched but faint sustained strings give an ethereal air to the piece, and this tranquility is pushed even further with the harp pattern beneath it. All of this is contextualized at 0:19 by a beautifully restrained yet emotional cello. The sparseness of the piece is part of the charm; it doesn’t need any embellishments to make you feel at ease, if not a bit reflective. Slight pitch bends add more color and if you rest long enough, a repressed high-register violin joins at 1:07 as a perfect ending.



“Fungal Wastes”, “Crystal Peak”, and “Resting Grounds” are all different area themes that I could cover in length but I just want to highlight the range of sound that Hollow Knight consistently manages to deliver. 

 

Crystal Peak is a large area befitting an almost spacey and vacant track. An echoing harp is the only musical line at the start, while the rest of the mix consists of synthetic whistles and sustained mechanical pitches. 1:37 breaks this mold with a bell ostinato figure and melody before gradually crescendoing and adding in some percussion. You’ll slowly start to hear an acoustic guitar enter the mix before 2:39 where a bass guitar cranks up the intensity with a neat bassline. The piece takes on a tribal drum ring sound before bringing in the original harp line on top as the piece reaches its full instrumentation for the area.

 

The Fungal Wastes are a mushroom-filled land which the piece evokes with its bouncy rhythms and playful instrumentation. Pizzicato strings fade in and out during the track, acting as the bass line and countermelody. At 0:12, you may even hear a familiar leitmotif that we haven't labeled yet, so hang in there. On top of these figures we also get a clarinet melody at 0:37, followed later at 1:25 by the oboe. 

 

The Resting Grounds are a land of spirits yet to move on, and you're likely to find yourself there by accident. As you might expect, a lone piano is the perfect fit. After a wandering piano ostinato and sustained strings introduce the track, the main melody arrives at 0:22. The ostinato pattern continues to play, just on the left hand instead of the higher register. The melody jumps between different octaves, while the string chords underneath provide just enough variation to keep the piece engaging while maintaining its thoughtful and sorrowful nature.



“Mantis Lords” is the boss theme named after the encounter that is equal parts intense and spectacular. The first thing that stands out is that the piece kicks off with a blazingly fast harpsichord line which is already extremely distinctive. This combines with aggressive low strings and fast moving violins which ramp up into the main idea at 0:12. The whole piece is built around call and response, with the violins initiating a phrase before the cellos answer with their own. The harpsichord alternates between accenting one of the two lines to focus your ear and provide unique accents. This pattern is interrupted at moments like 0:17 and 0:27, where the instruments play in unison for a powerful switch-up. At 0:43 we have a bridge where this call and response interaction is even more explicit and the violins get even more freedom to go all out. 

 

What's so brilliant about this piece's structure is how beautifully it mirrors the encounter itself. You’re fighting against the three mantis lords, but not in an unfair manner; the first one challenges you alone. You get to learn the moveset, you are the response to the call of the first mantis lord. Once you beat the first though, you’ll be faced with the remaining two mantis lords at the same time. These two opponents always repeat the exact same move as the previous one, more strongly enforcing the idea that the music has already been laid out. The hectic bridge section also works here: while the two Mantis Lords echo each other, you are the odd bug out as you try to match their rhythm. The music and boss design are brilliantly executed, with the theme perfectly accentuating what the fight is about. Absolute cinema. 

 


“City of Tears” is a gorgeous area theme not just for its composition, but also the context in which you experience it while playing the game. I’ll cover it more later, but reaching this location for the first time is when the game truly opens up and the music reflects that quite well. 

 

We start out with a harp ostinato figure that is soon joined by a beautiful vocal melody.This legato, or drawn-out, soprano tone is so soothing that you can really appreciate the presence of a real vocalist rather than a virtual instrument. At 0:24 some delicate sustained strings join in before low notes in the piano at 0:40 preempt the climax of the melodic line with a perfect high note. We then move into the next section where more harmonies and a choir are added to support the piece. At 1:10 the melody gets handed off to the flute which plays an alternating pattern with the harp before moving into 1:40. Here, the cello reiterates the main theme, with string stabs providing forward momentum while the choir continues to pad out the long chord progressions. 

 

Overall, this is just a phenomenally emotional track that lands at a perfect time — entering the gate and seeing a city with a permanent downpour could be emotionally dampening, but the warmth of the soundtrack and wealth of options provided to your exploration end up making this reveal one of the most memorable in the game.



“Soul Sanctum” is the theme for the sub-area within the City of Tears. This song has the tall task of musically conveying what happens when a group of scholars inhabiting a beautiful city become twisted by their ambition, cloistering themselves away to perform blasphemous experiments on life itself.

 

The answer is clearly to use an organ, which starts out the piece with a repeated figure that mirrors the ostinato from before but is a touch darker. Lower octave whole notes make the line more interesting by progressing the track through different minor chords every two bars.  At 0:43, the exact same melody from the City of Tears returns on the organ, providing a sense of familiarity because you're still in the city, but with a more sinister tone due to the unique chords driven by the pads and moving pattern underneath. "Varying the chord progression beneath an ostinato allows the same melody to be placed in different contexts. In this case we start in C Minor until the modulations have us placed in the darker B flat Minor as the piece progresses.



Soul Master, the boss of the area, takes over the piece at 2:16 with much more intensity. Heavy bowing in the low strings and strong percussion hits raise the stakes, while choral stabs cap off the epic feeling. 2:36 brings in the main melody again, this time in the choir with the low strings taking the ostinato figure. This pattern still acts as our chord progression and drives the piece forward. And wow, the comforting soprano melody feels so different in this context while still connecting to the area as a whole. This and the choir all crescendo into 3:08 where high string runs and powerful syncopated (offbeat) stabs pack a massive punch. This figure and the brass build toward the climax at 3:24, an intense string bridge supported by a demonic choir and heavy brass hits on the downbeat of every other bar. This then moves into 3:40 for a variation on the main melody with the brass section leading while the ostinato underneath keeps building the chord progression into 3:56 when the high strings take over the line for one last phrase. 

 

This is an absolutely insane piece that perfectly encapsulates the overwhelming atmosphere created by Hollow Knight's soundtrack. Your wonder and curiosity take you to the open City of Tears, where a heartfelt track welcomes you to a bounty of new opportunities. As that exploration leads you into the Soul Sanctum, the music slowly twists the theme you've grown to love into something more sinister. The sub-area then wraps up with an epic battle against the bug responsible for the corruption of soul in the area. The gameplay and music operate in perfect harmony to make all of these moments work.



“Broken Vessel” is the piece for the boss of the same name and it’s finally time for us to discuss the first leitmotif from earlier: the vessel leitmotif. The opening cutscene about entering Hallownest and the title theme both complement this track and explicitly — or as clearly as music can convey — tie themselves to the concept of vessels in the game. The title 'Broken Vessel' is pretty explicit, and the dream version of this boss is called 'Lost Kin,' so the connection is already on the nose. So what does this piece do with the leitmotif from before?

 

The original figure (think of the title theme) is transformed into a quarter note ostinato in a march style, just listen to those first five notes at 0:13! The rhythm is stretched out to even quarter notes and played by a low register cello and sounds absolutely evil. The melody comes in at 0:23 with the motif again, this time at the original rhythm, but with some changed notes afterward. The next line also mirrors the original motif, but with some different notes, leaving plenty of musical space for the idea to breathe. The track intentionally gives your ear time to process this concept and gain more familiarity with a narratively important theme. The emphasis on low strings and the macabre march also turn the theme into a dark boss-battle piece, rather than a gloomy but inquisitive theme.



Both “White Lady” and “White Palace” are narratively significant area themes due to the characters they represent. Right off the bat, 'White Lady' starts with the Pale leitmotif from 'Elegy for Hallownest,' which we previously discussed as being hidden in 'Hornet.' Given that Hornet is the daughter of the White Lady and Pale King, it makes sense for this theme to appear in both of these areas.

 

Aside from using the leitmotif as its main melody, 'White Lady' also features rolling harp chords as its moving line. The motif goes through some variations as the harp guides the track through different chord progressions, keeping the piece dynamic but still perfectly thematic for the narrative moment.

 

“White Palace” is a more complex track that opens up with a moving harp line while we get low chords in the cello and suspended notes in the violins. 0:44 the pale leitmotif comes in strong as the main melody played by the flute while the strings continue to support and drive the track forward. At 1:13 harp and violin double each other with the same theme melody while cello plays a mournful countermelody. This builds into 1:53 where we hear descending harp runs with string pads for chord progression. We get a peaceful bridge at 2:16 where the English horn and flute make an appearance with a call and response as the piece deviates from the original theme. High and low strings follow up as a call and response leading into the much darker toned passage at 2:56. The low cello register takes on the main theme doubled by the harp which makes for a more foreboding variation on the pale leitmotif; after all, the kingdom that once was is no longer and the music aids in painting that collapse over time.

 


“Truth, Beauty, and Hatred” on the other hand doesn’t have much to do with the narrative because it’s the theme for the dream fight against Zote. Though Zote himself is a cowardly character with no combat prowess, fighting the magnanimous version of his ego within his dreams flips the script and makes him quite the force to be reckoned with. Even though he is stronger, the fight features intentionally clumsy, difficult-to-read patterns that reflect this portrayal of incompetence which can be more than a little frustrating. All that being said, the theme makes no such parody and fully commits to a valiant, heroic theme packed with energy and forward momentum that fuels you to keep going. 

 

We immediately start with a high tempo moving eighth note line in the strings before the violin soars in the main melody. This line is filled with sixteenth note embellishments like at 0:14 and 0:19 that add to the royal quality of the song. At 0:28 the piece continues to up the scale of grandeur with the french horns taking the lead with some light, woodwind countermelodies. 0:41 gives the melody to the trumpets for more gusto while the violins double the line along with the piccolo way high up in the mix. 0:54 is a sort of bridge where we get brass stabs on the main beats while woodwinds play the melodic line. A quick flute run leads into a repeat of this passage but with trumpets taking over the melody before an insanely dense string run at 1:05 brings us into the second section. A lovely string melody carries the piece to 1:20 where the brass instruments build a chord progression into the final section at 1:26. 

 

The bass instruments all play a commanding quarter note march with heavy accents to create unstoppable momentum. The woodwinds play a call and response melody with enough room to increase the volume over the course of the whole passage. Strings tremolos get more intense while the marcato bass notes get even punchier. This is followed by a string run that leads into the climax at 1:39, accentuating all the components we've heard so far. The string bass joins the quarter note bassline while violin and trumpet double for the melody prompting a French horn countermelody alongside fortissimo violin tremolos. After two cycles of this, the trumpets join the countermelody to add even more impact before wrapping up the piece as an absolute slammer. 



Both “Grimm” and “Nightmare King” are boss themes from the Grimm Troupe DLC for the first and second fight against Troupe Master Grimm. They are also the only songs in the entire soundtrack that use electric guitar and drum kit. This gives them a notably different feel than the string and piano filled music found throughout the rest of the game, which fits the intrusive nature of the troupe’s arrival. 

 

“Grimm” starts out with a vampiric feeling organ figure while the choir and brass accent the strong beats of each phrase. 0:17 brings in the brass to take the melody while the organ creates the harmony and low strings play a moving line at the end of each bar. This idea repeats at 0:31, when the organ takes the main melody and the brass play descending lines and the violins play a small flourish at the end of each measure. 

 

All of these switches keep the piece interesting, like the high string runs leading up to 0:51 where a high French horn note kicks off a full ensemble passage, with all the instruments playing in harmony. These chords are separated by accented brass runs while they build up into a beat switch at 1:05. The same melodic pattern from the beginning is now sung by the choir while a distorted electric guitar and drumset establish a strong rock groove. 1:22 brings in the french horn melody while the choir supports the progression with quarter notes that emphasize the first beat. Layering the instrumentation and passing around the melody on top of the powerful rhythm section keeps this piece feeling energetic yet intimidating which is perfect for this battle — in both gameplay and the music it is a true dance.

 

“Nightmare King Grimm” takes us into the harder, second iteration of this boss battle after you’ve finished his questline. We start out with a different organ pattern that is more heavily accented by the brass and choir. This builds into 0:37 where we get the original melody once again while the drums drive the whole piece forward. Strings and brass take different melodies and rhythms throughout the theme to keep the familiar back and forth going. At 1:31, we enter a sharp, dissonant vocal interlude with brass stabs that increase the tension. The intensity continues to build until 2:12 where a lyrical passage gives more time for the dance to really shine. The slowed down feeling and sustained chords from the organ and choir give the section a feeling of grandeur that is fitting for the dream — or nightmare — version of this fight.



“Daughter of Hallownest”, “Sisters of Battle”, and “Pure Vessel” are all arrangements of earlier songs from the game, heard during battles in the Godhome DLC. The first two don’t stray too far from the original versions, mostly opting for more dramatic instrumentation with a full choir and more brass to pack a punch. We actually haven't covered the third piece yet, and I'll discuss it right after this section; it deserves to be the final track we talk about, trust me.



“Daughter of Hallownest” is the godhome version of Hornet’s theme. Flute and vocals are now the focus of the melody with support from the string section. At 0:16 the choir takes the melody until the next section where it doubles the strings for the strong beats like 0:31. Trumpet accents make the song less delicate and more of an epic at 0:52. At 1:11 the french horn and choral line inspire grand feelings before a battle between the flutes and trumpets mirrors the call and response of “Hornet”. The rapid sixteenth-note string lines at 1:30 are the same as in the original, but now feature choir harmonies, a horn countermelody, and spiccato strings to accentuate the arrangement's epic nature.



“Sisters of Battle” adds more brass and choir to the ‘Mantis Lords’ piece, adding impact through strong-beat accents and fanfare. You hear this right away: a choral stab at the beginning, followed by marching brass quarter notes at 0:08 and trumpet flourishes shortly afterward. At 0:14 female vocals take the response melody while at 0:23 the male vocals double the melody. All of the instrumentation in this song adds intensity and scale compared to the original.

 

The battle matches this musical escalation. Even though the first phase is the same, you fight all three Mantis Lords at once instead of just facing the remaining two together. This is an incredible fight with a ton of energy in both the combat and soundtrack.



“Pure Vessel” is a rearrangement of “Sealed Vessel”, which we’ll cover at the end of this really long analysis section. This piece is a much fuller composition compared to the imperfect version, with fuller instrumentation and fewer dissonant chords. The instrumentation is more noble as well with the choir, woodwinds, and tubular bells. At 0:25 we get the familiar run but in the woodwinds this time while brass, choir, and high strings support. Instead of an air of suffering and tragedy though, this piece is more regal and inevitable. 

 

The rhythm never breaks down into the repetitive cycle of the first theme, reflecting the ideal world that could have been if the Hollow Knight had been truly pure. The track builds into 1:02 with a soaring tenuto passage doubled by the bells. The delicate intensity of this composition makes it feel more like a choral rite or prayer welcoming a holy being than the soundtrack to a decaying bastion of the old world. Speaking of that, let’s move on to our last piece.



“Sealed Vessel” is the theme for the Hollow Knight itself (yeah you don’t play as the hollow knight, sorry) and my favorite song in the game. It has so much weight, both musically and narratively, including a payoff for all the leitmotifs that you’ve gotten accustomed to over the course of the whole game. So let’s dive in.

 

After a long string tremolo buildup starting at 0:22, the piece kicks off at 0:43 with so much intensity. We have energetic runs in the low strings and a marching quarter-note rhythm in the bells, which is then doubled by the high strings and, afterward, the violin. You’ll recognize that the rhythm actually starts out with the vessel leitmotif! At 0:59 we hear a crazy sixteenth note violin line countered by a mirrored run in the cello. This accompanies the main melody towards the back of the mix which is again our vessel leitmotif stretched out into half notes, but played by strings, vocals, and bells. We then reach 1:39 where we continue to ramp up the impact as brass hits mark the beginning of each phrase in addition to some more soaring runs. At 1:55, the piece breaks down, leading into the classic boss screech and then phase two.

 

2:15 is the moment of truth where the narrative truly comes to life as the Hollow Knight gains awareness of their surroundings and starts to stab themselves. This twist from a gameplay perspective is of course matched in the soundtrack as the music drastically changes to a somber tone. This section starts with a string quartet playing the same theme heard in both Path of Pain and Birthplace, both of which are present in the White Palace. This immediately establishes another musical connection to the relationship between the Vessels and the Pale King.

 

This passage is basically an endless loop of the vessel leitmotif that never actually resolves, instead landing on the tonic to restart the cycle. Sound familiar? The first two beats of the ¾ phrase are a repeated note that gets harmonized by cello and viola for a deeply mournful sound. Each phrase lasts eleven bars instead of a more even twelve, which further emphasizes the tension and lack of finality that the story is trying to convey. 

 

A violin solo eventually joins at 2:51, accompanied by a constantly ascending chord progression, even though the rhythm continues to repeat. The choir backs this up with vocals at 3:06 that also build up throughout the passage. The section at 3:27 repeats the entire phrase; then, at 3:44, the brass starts accentuating the ends of the measures. All of this newly built-up instrumentation continues to crescendo together, creating a remarkable pressure cooker of emotion ready to overflow. 

 

As the fight concludes at 4:05, we transition into the ending cutscene — which varies between the standard and true endings — and the vocals take over the vessel leitmotif as the melody. The low strings accompany this with an eight-note descending pattern that leads down to the tonic (the first note of the scale) of A minor. At 4:14, a sweeping brass run punctuates the passage with raw emotion, while a countermelody in the horns plays a more grand variation of the vessel leitmotif above the mix. 

 

Now if after all this you’ve gone for the true ending,you'll hear the same figure from the game's opening cutscene at 4:37, while a lone, sustained violin note fades beneath it. Then, at 4:59, the flute plays the Pale leitmotif as the melody, while the piano plucks out a countermelody using the vessel leitmotif, reflecting the fulfillment of the Pale King's dream. 

 

5:13 brings us the final melody in none other than the viola that started it all. We hear the vessel leitmotif one final time, but now it is slightly different. At the end of the melody, we finally get to hear the phrase continue beyond the first four notes of the leitmotif, which we've only heard in the opening cutscene. The melody wraps up on the tonic, finally resolving for the very first time after not doing that for the entire game! It's an expression of utter relief and is such a satisfying payoff after a magnificent game.

Content

One of the first things that makes Hallownest immersive is Cornifer, the cartographer you can find humming as he works. He's a slightly cowardly but determined little weevil who has made mapping the kingdom his life's work. His maps are useful, of course, but the process of finding him becomes a rewarding part of exploration in its own right.

When you enter a new area, you’ll often be lost and unsure of where to go. Finding Cornifer gives you a tangible goal while you're still trying to understand your surroundings, and naturally leads into further exploration. His humming becomes a familiar sound, and discovering him feels like finding a friendly face in an unfamiliar place. His wife Iselda's affectionate nickname for him, “Corny,” also adds personality to their relationship. This becomes even more apparent when you visit their house in Dirtmouth and are greeted by an unenthused “bapanada” from Iselda. It’s the little moments of charm that add up to make the decrepit world of Hollow Knight feel so lively.

Cornifer is a small example of how Hollow Knight turns a practical game mechanic into part of its worldbuilding. The map isn't just a menu you fill out; rather, it's something another character is creating alongside you. His presence makes the act of charting Hallownest more intimate, and his vivid descriptions help make the places you visit feel like somewhere he has actually been.

 

Greenpath is another early demonstration of how effectively the game establishes the identity of a region. After the muted atmosphere of the Forgotten Crossroads, the lush greenery and distinctive soundtrack immediately make it feel different.

The transition makes the world feel expansive before you've even traveled very far. You're not simply entering the next level. You're discovering another corner of a kingdom with its own atmosphere and character.

 

The introduction to the City of Tears takes that even further. Before reaching it, you encounter clues that suggest its importance. Posted signs hint at the existence of a great city, while a strange, masked bug named Quirrel advises you to head there to upgrade your weapon. Once you best the Mantis Lords in combat (a quintessentially perfect boss fight, by the way), you’ll unlock the wall climb and finally be able to enter the city.

Suddenly you're standing in an enormous room with rain falling from above. The music for the first time swells with soaring vocals. The environment is unlike anything the game has shown you before, with its massively tall buildings and torrent of rain. As you walk around, you'll quickly find the linear structure of the game fading away, with a myriad of new paths opening up for exploration. 

This moment is so special because it truly feels earned — the attention to detail throughout the early areas built up anticipation while your curiosity led you to a beautiful place that feels deeply rewarding. Not because you earned some tangible reward and not because the game even told you to go there. The experience is so memorable because the game makes you feel as though you've discovered something fantastic for yourself.

 

Deepnest shows the other side of that curiosity. You can stumble into it by falling through a collapsing floor, and the transition is startling. The environment is dark, cramped, and hostile. The soundtrack drops out, replaced by faint drones, chittering creatures, and echoing groans that make the space feel deeply unsettling.

The lack of a map at the entrance makes the experience even more disorienting. You don't have the comfort of Cornifer's familiar humming to guide you to a map, and the enemies can feel overwhelming when you're underprepared and still trying to work out where you are.

This is a great example of Hollow Knight using the player's expectations against them. By this point, you're accustomed to exploring new areas and searching for their secrets. Deepnest turns that instinct into something uncomfortable. You still want to know what's ahead, but now you're also afraid of finding out.

The fake Knight and the sequence leading to Nosk are particularly effective. The idea that you might be following something familiar, only to discover that it isn't what it appears to be, makes the area feel actively hostile and disturbing. While the Nosk boss fight itself is less compelling to me, the overall encounter is memorable because of what the game puts you through during the buildup. Fear of the unknown is certainly powerful enough to dissuade recklessness and make you leave a note to return later.

 

The entrance to the Hive is a small but wonderful example of how Hollow Knight trains you to pay attention. Within Kingdom's Edge, a breakable wall hides a small part of the Hive that soon dead ends. Near the start of the area, though, two bees seem oddly placed. If you attack them near the wall or use your spells, you may discover that the wall itself is breakable and that an entire area lies behind it. That is the attention to detail and inspired design that I love about Hollow Knight. You spend hours in the game, subtly being taught how to look at your surroundings and learn. Eventually, the world feels more like a real place rather than just a scenic background.

 

The same principle applies to rescuing the 46 captured Grubs throughout the game. At first, rescuing one is simply checking off a satisfying secret (and saving an incredibly cute creature). Saving a few more shortly after becomes a way to get rewarded with money and upgrades. Eventually, saving them all can easily become a personal mission.

Their little cries, their reactions when rescued, and the knowledge that they are scattered throughout the entire kingdom of Hallownest transforms what could have been a simple collectible into something you genuinely care about completing. The game doesn't need to explicitly tell you that these creatures are your friends. It creates an atmosphere where you will develop that attachment yourself.

 

Moving towards the action, the Mantis Lords perfectly demonstrate the core principles behind combat in Hollow Knight: the attacks are readable, the patterns give room for counterplay, and the arena gives you just enough room to operate. You have the dash at this point, which makes your movement feel fluid, and can even use downward strikes to bounce off enemies or hazards — the technique I alluded to earlier commonly called “pogoing”. This mechanic adds an exceptional amount of depth to the core movement of the game and is insanely fun to use.

Back to the fight though — once you find the rhythm, the fight becomes an immensely satisfying dance. The nature of the fight adds to this feeling; you’re not attacking a mindless beast, you’re challenging one of the final bastions resisting the infection plaguing the kingdom. This is where the precision of Hollow Knight's controls is front and center. You're not fighting the controls or getting hit when you don't expect to; you just need to continuously improve at mastering the tools given to you.

 

The Colosseum of Fools takes those same mechanics and pushes them into a different context. In its more demanding trials, the arena itself becomes part of the challenge. Floorless sections, spikes, and waves of enemies force you to use pogoing, wall jumps, dashes, and enemy positioning to stay alive.

The result is a hybrid of combat encounter and platforming puzzle. You aren't simply trying to hit enemies as quickly as possible; you're figuring out how to move through a space where every mistake can leave you in danger.

The second and third trials also introduce memorable boss encounters, making the Colosseum a great endgame showcase for the range of challenges Hollow Knight can build from its basic mechanics.

 

Path of Pain is a hidden, optional platforming challenge that takes Hollow Knight's movement mechanics to their extremes. It's a reminder of just how much precision the game has built into its seemingly simple controls. This challenge requires you to execute a difficult sequence of simple movements with immaculate consistency. In that way, it very much reminds me of Celeste. It certainly doesn't reach the same level of difficulty as some of that game's later content, but it still demands a level of control that the main game never requires.

What makes Path of Pain work is that it feels like a test of everything you've learned. It doesn't need to introduce a new moveset. It takes the tools you already understand and asks you to use them at a higher level. Since it’s fully optional, it also lets players decide how far they want to push their mastery.

 

Nightmare King Grimm is another excellent example of Hollow Knight increasing the difficulty without abandoning fairness. His attacks are faster and more demanding than those in your first “dance,” with additional effects and variations that force you to react more precisely.

The full-screen title card makes it clear that you're entering a major challenge. From there, the fight becomes a process of learning the rhythm of his attacks, finding safe opportunities to respond, and gradually making fewer mistakes.

The game doesn't need to give you a new set of tools to make the encounter challenging. It only needs to demand more from the tools you already have. The fight is difficult because it expects you to cleanly execute at a high level, not because it discards the rules you've spent the game learning.

 

The Pantheons introduced through the game's DLC already provide demanding sequences of encounters, including new bosses. The Pantheon of Hallownest takes that idea further, challenging you to conquer the game's full roster of bosses in sequence, along with some additional encounters.

The difficulty comes not only from any individual fight but from the need to remain consistent across a long series of them. You have to adapt to different patterns, manage your resources, and avoid letting one mistake snowball into a run-ending failure.

It's a culmination of Hollow Knight's combat design. By this point, the game isn't asking you to discover an entirely new mechanic. It's asking you to demonstrate how well you've mastered everything that came before. Streaming my eventual victory in this test of strength and endurance was an awesome experience, especially knowing that it would unlock the game's final ending and reveal even more lore.

 

Not every reward in Hollow Knight has to make you stronger or provide a moment of excitement. Sitting with Quirrel at the Blue Lake is one of the quietest moments in the game, and one of the ones that has stuck with me the most. After spending so much time navigating dangerous environments and fighting through Hallownest, you simply sit beside someone you've met along the way and look out over the calming blue water.

There's also a lovely environmental connection: the water pooled in the Blue Lake is what eventually leaks out and forms the rain falling over the City of Tears. A small detail like that reinforces the feeling that Hallownest is one interconnected place, even when its regions look and feel very different. 

It's a peaceful moment of reflection that enhances the journey you've been on. Quirrel isn't just an NPC who gives you information. He's someone you've encountered throughout your travels, and sharing this quiet moment with him makes the world feel personal. Even though the moment is likely a sendoff to a friend you won't see again, that brief respite creates a memory that will last forever. Hollow Knight is phenomenal at understanding that sometimes the best reward for exploring somewhere is simply being allowed to stop and appreciate where you've ended up.

 

The Stag's Nest provides a similar kind of emotional payoff toward the end of the game. After spending the entire game relying on the Last Stag as a familiar and trustworthy means of fast travel, discovering his hidden home feels like finding a piece of the world that existed beyond your own journey. His grunts and shuffling have become synonymous with a familiar sense of duty at the end of the world, so imagine the surprise when this beetle mentions that they’ve found a new location to travel to. 

Even more surprising is the miraculous discovery you make upon inspecting a certain object: “A broken egg. It is empty.” Talking to your friend once more gives him hope that a new generation of stags has ventured out into the world, even changing their screen name from "The Last Stag" to "The Old Stag". "After spending so much of the game surrounded by decay and loss, finding a fragment of hope for new life feels wonderful without requiring fanfare.

 

The Birthplace sequence is one of the most important moments in Hollow Knight because it changes how you understand the Knight, the Hollow Knight, and Hallownest itself.

Descending into the Abyss reveals more about the origin of the Vessels and the history behind the kingdom's desperate attempts to contain the infection. The scene with the Pale King discarding you and leaving with the Hollow Knight communicates a great deal through imagery rather than direct explanation.

The return journey is just as powerful. As you climb back out, dream dialogue repeats the adage introduced earlier in the game. What initially seemed like an enigmatic phrase now carries a different meaning because you understand more of the history behind it.

This is where Hollow Knight's environmental storytelling and gameplay come together most effectively. You're not simply watching a cutscene that explains the past. You're moving through a place that embodies it, and the physical act of descending into the Abyss and climbing back out becomes part of how you experience the narrative.

The scene also gives the Pale King's actions a tragic dimension. His decision to leave with the Hollow Knight feels like a choice made under the shadow of an ending he cannot escape. The game doesn't need to spell out every detail for the image to communicate helplessness, regret, and the terrible cost of his plan.

 

The first look at the Radiance is one of the most insane moments in the base game. For most of your journey, the infection is this strange, almost abstract force hanging over Hallownest. You've seen its manifestations in the orange goop that plagues the forgotten husks around the kingdom, and you've also heard of it as the light that can consume a bug's mind. 

Eventually, entering the mind of the Hollow Knight places you in a somewhat serene location: an eternally bright sky with just the sun in view. That all changes when you approach the sun and see the text “Challenge,” just as you did when facing the Mantis Lords early on. 

The idea that the game's ultimate threat is effectively the sun is an unimaginably hype reveal. The Radiance is a godlike creature tied to the moths you've encountered throughout the game, and the final obstacle (of the base game, at least) standing in your way of the true ending.

 

One of my favorite moments in the entire game comes during the fight against the Hollow Knight.

I was watching a friend play when the boss reached its second phase and began stabbing itself. My friend asked, “Wait, why is he doing that... Oh... I understand.” 

That moment captures exactly what makes Hollow Knight's storytelling so effective. The Hollow Knight looks enough like the Knight that the resemblance is immediately apparent, but the meaning of its actions only becomes clear when you consider what you've learned about the Vessels and the infection.

At first, the self-stabbing might seem like an opportunity to get some free hits in. Then the realization sets in: the Hollow Knight isn't simply fighting you. It's suffering, and it is turning its weapon against itself to try and help you.

 

Another friend of mine cried during this encounter, and I can understand why. The game has spent so long building its world and revealing its history in fragments that a single action can communicate what a lengthy exposition scene might struggle to achieve. You understand the tragedy not because a character explains it, but because you have enough context to recognize what you're seeing. 

It's an emotional payoff for the game's atmosphere, worldbuilding, and storytelling. The Hollow Knight is no longer just a being you have to defeat to save the world. It becomes a figure whose fate reflects the larger tragedy of Hallownest.

 

Even in a decaying world with an oppressive atmosphere, Hollow Knight creates such a strong mood that it's difficult not to feel something while playing. The sense of discovery, whether through the narrative or the gameplay, makes an abandoned kingdom feel alive. And above all, it creates that incredible feeling of finding something new and being hungry to find what's ahead.

Sometimes that discovery is beautiful; other times, it's unsettling. Sometimes it's a mechanically demanding encounter; other times, it's a heartbreaking moment. Sometimes it's simply a quiet place for reflection; other times, it's a jovial cry from a bug that you'll repeat to your friends. Hollow Knight continually gives you reasons to wonder what is around the next corner and makes you believe that whatever you find will be worth discovering.

Summary

Hollow Knight is one of those rare games where the experience is truly magical in a way that is difficult to describe. Its precise controls make combat and platforming satisfying to master, its interconnected world is densely packed with secrets and meaningful rewards, and its art, music and characters give Hallownest a remarkably strong atmosphere.

What makes the game special, though, is how these strengths reinforce one another. Curiosity draws you into the world, progression fuels that intrigue, and the mechanics make acting on that drive satisfying. The atmosphere, characters, and environment contextualize that discovery in a way that makes even a small secret or quiet moment memorable 

The convergence of all those systems gives Hollow Knight a rich atmosphere that oozes with personality and consistently creates the feeling that something wonderful is ahead.
