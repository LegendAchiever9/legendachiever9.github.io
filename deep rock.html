Deep Rock Galactic

Played in 2023


Elevator Pitch

Lead a dwarven mining expedition in this cooperative shooter where every mission becomes its own story through procedurally generated caves with destructible terrain. Choose between four classes and customize your loadout with unique tools (and maybe a beer) that let you and your friends play your own way. Rock and Stone!

Content

Deep Rock Galactic is a first person horde shooter where up to four players will dive into the core of Hoxxes IV to complete missions for big rig mining employers. Each adventure takes place in randomly generated caves alongside many other systems that promote variability. The game doesn’t have this randomness just to increase playtime though, it instead synergizes with the game’s design to create unique situations that players must react to on the fly.

 

One mission dropped us into a cave that was an enormous descending labyrinth. By the time we'd reached the bottom, three bosses had spawned in the same room while a swarm of enemies arrived and we were nearly out of ammunition. Fifteen frantic minutes later we were still running laps around the cave trying to revive each other. No matter what the mission objective actually was, I absolutely remember that cave and our experience to get through it.



Even with procedurally generated environments, the cave within the same mission is never a static obstacle. Whether through your trusty pickaxe, massive drills, explosives, or burrowing enemies, almost all terrain in Deep Rock is fully destructible. This grants players the agency to turn impossible spaces into manageable ones or forge their own path to explore the vastness of the alien environments they find themselves in.

 

One of my favorite memories comes from using that freedom for absolutely no tactical purpose. During a quiet moment while we were excavating a crystalline cavern, I drilled a tunnel straight down while my teammate was busy mining. I then drilled my way back up and nudged them into the hole as we all erupted in laughter while he fell to the bottom. The unique cave structure and terraforming aren't what make Deep Rock Galactic tick. They're tools that augment everything else the game does to create unforgettable expeditions with your friends. Speaking of  which, let’s get into them!



There are a variety of mechanics in Deep Rock that change from mission to mission, which in turn constantly create new scenarios. Mission types, secondary objectives, biome, mutators, enemies, teammates, builds, classes, and hazard levels are all variables that facilitate replayability. Customizing your own loadout for each of the four classes allows each mission to feel fresh while being able to alter the hazard level, or difficulty, of each mission makes the game highly approachable for all types of players.

 

I’ll talk about specific enemies, biomes, and mutators more after the point of no return — there aren’t any real spoilers to this game, more so just my opinions on the content — so definitely read that if you want to see more examples of how Deep Rock’s gameplay mechanics interact with one another to create a whole greater than the sum of its parts.



Classes are differentiated not just by their damage output and weapon types, but with movement abilities. Drilling tunnels to create shortcuts, creating platforms, setting up zipline escape routes, and swinging through the caves with a grappling hook are the cornerstone traversal tools that help define your playstyle. For every situation you run into, each player thinks about how their class can solve the problem — everyone can contribute to a predicament in their own way.

 

And while I mention teammates as a variable, it’s worth saying that coop is definitely my preferred way to enjoy Deep Rock. Working as a team, especially through the harder content, feels great and creates highly memorable moments. 

For example, near the end of one mission two of us sprinted toward the extraction point and “accidentally” left our last teammate behind. They went down, and what should have been a routine extraction instantly became a timed rescue mission. Those sudden shifts from relaxed banter to coordinated panic are where Deep Rock Galactic shines brighter than a compressed gold chunk.



In terms of progression, Deep Rock has a compelling amount of content to aim for with the class prestige system, performance pass (a free battlepass for resources that help you advance), a breadth of overclocks to further customize your weapons, and the “raid tier” of missions called deep dives. All of this encourages you to engage with the game by fulfilling the secondary objectives on each mission, defeating optional bosses, and pursuing more difficult hazard levels. The game provides compelling reasons to keep returning to its excellent mission generation, while constantly giving you new ways to approach them.

 

Once you level a class up high enough you unlock deep dives, which are effectively a miniature roguelite raid mission that is a step up in challenge compared to the rest of the game. You’ll be tasked with completing three consecutive missions that exceed the highest hazard level, with the same resource pool, and each one has at least one mutator. The added challenge and longer term structure makes these some of my favorite missions in the whole game — while the normal missions are often happy to let you relax and enjoy the chaos, deep dives are where the game starts demanding the planning and coordination that its systems have been preparing you for.



Speaking of completing content, I’ll briefly talk about the optionally purchasable cosmetic content. The entire performance pass is completely free and unlocked just by playing the game. The only thing you can even spend real money on is non-gameplay DLC that has some cosmetic content to support the developers. Additionally, there is no rush on players to collect everything in one season — all previous battle pass content and even special event content in Deep Rock Galactic remains accessible just by playing. This is a rare gem of a monetization system in today's landscape and an extremely welcome one; now, back to the game!



The game also has a phenomenally social community. The classic chant of "Rock and Stone", buying beers for your fellow dwarves, and a whole host of funny voice lines encourages players to lean into the silliness. Deep Rock Galactic doesn't force a community onto you, it actually manages to create a wholesome and authentic environment that makes players want to participate in. That leads to community made memes like spam-pinging “mushroom” and “we’re rich” when you see a mushroom or gold chunk, respectively in a mission. There was even a prop hunt gamemode in the hub that our group played for quite a while, and I will say that I found a miraculously effective spot. Deep Rock gives players an invitation to participate in the goofy environment rather than forcing a community onto them and it is remarkably effective.



A final point I’ll make for this section is the immaculate sound design in Deep Rock. We’ll of course talk about the dark synthwave soundtrack in detail during the music section, but the enemy sounds especially excel at both gameplay clarity and immersion. The guttural clicks of a Glyphid Menace unburrowing and high pitched cry of a Mactera Grabber are unmistakable which encourages players to stay alert and gather auditory information to prepare for what could be up ahead.




From debating who should cover gantry on the drilldozer to hectic team improvisation during an imminent hoard, Deep Rock Galactic excels at delivering both chill social moments and high octane gameplay. It is a wonderfully designed game that allows players to complete missions in their own way as a team.

Pain Points

There isn’t much to complain about in Deep Rock. Some missions can start to feel repetitive after one hundred hours, but between the different classes, customizations, and six seasons of free content updates, it will take quite a while before you possibly feel that fatigue.



If you play with a static group, you’re a bit discouraged from doing active missions on your own since that will get you out of sync from your team on mission progress. Since some advancements require you to complete several missions in a row, we occasionally had to do some “dead” runs if we didn’t have everyone. Not a huge deal, but something that came up for our group even though we played quite consistently.



Lastly in the progression space, getting a specific overclock relies on random drops which can be a  frustrating experience. These are gated to weekly pickups so it is possible to wait quite a while before you get an overclock for a class and weapon that you’re looking for. That being said, the game both expects and rewards players who cycle between the different classes, which is reasonable! That’s just not how I prefer to play, let me handle the digging:)

Music

We’ll start out with some of the ambient themes in the soundtrack which play during the periods of downtime in each mission. 

 

“Coward's Crossing” is our first background theme with some heavy bass in both the strings and synth. Intermittent stabs comprise the majority of the soundspace aside from light warbles and ethereal higher notes fluttering above. We have some percussion join in at 1:36 to bring some momentum into the piece while the synth and bass continue to pulse. At 2:33 we get a synth glissando that leads us into a dreamlike beat where a hollow sounding synth plays an ascending ostinato pattern with a deep string bass underneath. After a quieter section, this section comes back at 5:21, but the ascending pattern is much fuller. This takes us into a synth lead at 5:55 which carries the “melody” until 6:47 — the second half serves as an escalation of the earlier parts of the piece by using higher pitched synths and more moving lines to indicate your progression through the level.



“Echoes From The Past” is another atmospheric track that brings a much grander feeling to a pounding rhythm section and more defined melodies. We start out with an organ playing a dirge until 0:35 when war drums and throat chanting take over. The vocals take a quick syncopated beat to help keep the piece moving while we hear a somber synth lead at 0:52. After a lower action middle section, this synth melody comes back at 1:51 while the song escalates into 2:00 where the organ and vocals return along with harmonies and bass to provide a much heartier sound. We leave the climax at 2:33 when a crunchy bass takes full control of the melody, including a syncopated beat at 2:47 which mirrors the earlier chanting rhythm. 

 

Now lets move into the action with the swarm themes that trigger when you’ll soon be met by a host of bugs all honed in on your position.


“They're Here!” kicks kicks off with a percussion hit and fast moving synth bassline to keep the energy up as a high whistle soars over top. This crescendos into 0:50 where we drop the rhythm section and add some echoing pads in order to make room for a menacing synth and piano to take the melody. This continues into 1:08 where the melody pitch bends all the way down as the grumbling bass takes back over to drive the track again. This time with the added help of pulsing synths and a piano to add even more weight to the moment.



“A Distant Terror” hits you with a jaws-style intro with a dark synth and mechanical chitters. All the while, a heartbeat effect starts at 0:05 and continues through to 0:30 where you’ll hear a synth pattern start to play and grow. This crescendos until more sounds join in at 0:40 where we keep this twisted machination through a couple of rhythmic stabs. This all drops away at 1:13 when a deep robotic warbling takes over in addition to a wailing synth.



“RUN!” is an iconic theme that starts out with a lone robot-sounding synth setting an ostinato beat for the track. Percussion, bass, and echoing high synths join in to keep the piece moving into 0:45 where we slow down a bit for a synth interlude. 1:14  has these synths take the front while the rhythm section from earlier joins back in to bring back the groove. Using the M.U.L.E sound effects to create a dark techno track is so creative and renders this track as one of the most actively memorable, even without a strong melody!



“Axes Out” is the last swarm theme we’ll cover and it’s definitely one of the most melodic. We start out with some heavy synths before a fast moving line in the high synths joins in and cranks the energy up. All of the instrumentation cuts out at 0:26 to introduce the main bass line for this piece, a simple yet effective walking pattern with a groovy synth. A lighter, wood sounding synth and deeper bass take over this line while a low pitched marimba plays a countermelody of sorts and a pulsing synth acts as our rhythm section. This crescendos into 1:06 where we strip away most of the sounds for two synths to take the melody backed up by a drumset. Descending synth notes and a hi hat bring back the pace into a repeat of the key bass line, this time with different synths. Another great example of keeping the central motif familiar to your ear while giving it enough depth and complexity to be interesting to listen to throughout a whole swarm.

Music

Before you leave any mission you’ll need to defend the dropship landing zone while the extraction theme keeps you going. 

 

“Robot Getaway” slowly builds up with an ascending ostinato figure at 0:13, echoed by a deeper synth at 0:24. We get a percussion hit at 0:31 to begin a groove while we grow into 0:56 where a sustained synth note floats above the mix. The piece continues to crescendo before the drop at 1:28 where we get the full melody of the track. The high synth get into a call and response with itself while the lower bass accentuates the downbeats all while pulsating synths and the percussive hits keep the whole track moving. The slow and punchy melodic line with fast moving parts underneath is a perfect sound to highlight the return of the clunky M.U.L.E robot to the dropship.



“March of the Brave” starts out true to its name with a snare drum solo typical of marches while a deep synth mirrors this pattern. 0:10 brings out sustained strings and a full brass section; immediately this change to orchestral instrumentation already stands out. This avengers style slow crescendo. takes us into 0:35 where a melancholy synth takes the melody. 0:59 brings in a sitar stab until we hit the climax at 1:10 where the mix is at its fullest sound. We then have a longer quiet section to give the ears some room before ramping up again into a full sequence of stabs from the brass at 2:56. The brass briefly kick off the melody at 3:28 before the synth rejoins with a new, ascending phrase this time. The piece feels like an epic conclusion to an arduous battle that finally ends with you rising above the rubble.

Content

I mentioned earlier that there isn’t really anything to truly spoil in Deep Rock, so I’ll use this section to talk about the strength of variability that the game sports across enemies, biomes, mission types, and mutators. In isolation, none of these would individually allow the game to be as replayable as it is — but when they join together alongside the procedural terrain it enables Deep Rock to truly feel different in every run.

 

The enemy design throughout the game is fantastic, with a ton of creativity and even worldbuilding packed in every creature. I previously mentioned the exceptional audio design in Deep Rock, noting how the distinct sound cues perfectly accentuate the individual gameplay challenges that each creature introduces. While the flora and fauna of Hoxxes are unique on their own, the way they synergize with the other systems of the game is their strongest element. They both serve to boost the diverse experiences that you’ll encounter on every run as well as encourage customization, planning, and teamwork.

 

You’ve got the whale-like sounds of a Naedocyte breeder that echo through the cave on mission start, letting you know that these high health giants are present. Hearing these groans can turn any mission into a ‘find and kill’ for this organic hatchling factory that can easily snowball a situation out of control 

 

In the airborne Mactera family, the Tri-Jaw is a pretty tanky creature that packs an absolute punch. While an ill-timed barrage from a group of these bugs will almost immediately break your shields, they share the global weakness of this class of enemies to frost damage, so if you pack some ice weaponry ahead of time you’ll be ready. 

 

In the Glyphid class of enemies we have what is probably my least favorite enemy, the Septic Spreader. If left unchecked, a group of these is enough to cause a massive headache especially if you’re forced to play in a confined area due to either the mission objective or unhelpful terrain. Your squad will need excellent spatial awareness and diligence to not get caught off guard by these.

 

All hands on deck if you see a Bulk Detonator, as these enormous tanks threaten explosive attacks with a massive radius, even on defeat. Using your special weapons like C4 or kiting the bug to show its weak point to a gunner can bring it down quickly, so sync up with your teammates!

 

Moving on to an optional boss creature, the Korlok Tyrant Weed has possibly one of the most devastating difficulty ceilings of any creature on Hoxxes. This carnivorous plant has several pods dispersed from its core, and you must break all of them within a time frame to actually attempt to damage it. The burst window is pretty narrow so you’ll need your best damage and stellar timing to take this down in a reasonable amount of cycles. The catch is that heavily uneven or disruptive terrain can make this an absolute nightmare to deal with so you really won’t want to engage this battle before everyone is ready to go.

 

The enemies aren’t the only way that Deep Rock deepens the quality and variety of the experience though — there are eleven different biomes in the game that all have unique aspects that affect gameplay. 

 

Movement inhibiting locations like the fungus bogs and magma cavern force you to think about how to work around the usable space that you have available. The magma core also has earthquakes that make this even worse, especially during a swarm; fortunately the gunner’s ziplines or engineer’s platforms can mitigate these issues! 

 

The crystalline cavern has pale blue crystals that can chain electricity between them while the radioactive exclusion zone has large pillars that radiate harmful particles. Both of these obstacles can be smashed away by the driller to make the environment more suitable for the mission. The glacial strata and and sandblasted corridor sport ice and sand, respectively, that can be more easily mined by the standard pickaxe when compared to the standard rock and stone.  Extreme weather conditions like a sandstorm or blizzard will also periodically trigger, causing reduced visibility and even start to deal damage if your temperature drops enough. 

 

All of these biome-specific traits serve to throw an extra level of variability into each mission, forcing you to plan for, and interact with, the systems that heighten the experience of the game.



Mutators are yet another way that the game strives to keep missions fresh. Haunted cave has an invincible and translucent bulk detonator slowly following your crew throughout the whole mission, putting a soft timer on every action. Duck and cover is a ruthless modifier that replaces almost all enemies with ranged attackers; lethal enemies on the other hand, boosts melee damage of bugs by 50%. Low oxygen makes it so that you need to stay within close proximity to the M.U.L.E for life support, while shield disruption makes it so that you can’t generate any shields throughout the whole mission. These modifiers drastically change the lethality of the mission and require the player, especially on higher hazard levels, to play smarter instead of recklessly mowing through waves of enemies. The more benevolent low gravity mutator takes this to the extreme by fundamentally changing how you even move through the level, warping the stage into a new experience altogether.



Even the mission types themselves all lend themselves to different styles of play, whether building oil refinement rails that you can railgrind on or escorting the drilldozer to mine a precious gem deep into the cavern. The point extraction mission type can be pretty brutal if you don’t have vertical transport abilities on your squad, so come prepared! I have great memories of an enormously deep cavern where my team took turns trying to throwing aquarqs (heavy blue gems) down a huge drop to play makeshift basketball. Deep Rock is all about the fun you create inside a diverse sandbox, where every mechanic works with each other to support an overall experience that is a blast to play again and again.

Summary

Deep Rock Galactic succeeds because every one of its systems reinforces the same goal: making cooperative gameplay produce memorable stories. While there is already a strong game loop of a hectic horde shooter with a unique set of tools inside unpredictable caves, the game trusts that its design is compelling enough to let stories emerge naturally. Hundreds of hours later, I'm still remembering the stories more than the missions themselves. Rock! And! Stone!
